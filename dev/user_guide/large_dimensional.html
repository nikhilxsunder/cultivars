Large and high-dimensional systems#

An unrestricted VAR on \(k\) series with \(p\) lags has \(p k^2\) slope coefficients, and past a handful of variables a macroeconomic sample cannot pin them down. cultivars.multivariate.large_dim offers three answers: shrink every coefficient toward a prior (the Bayesian VARs, documented on Bayesian VARs), select a sparse support by penalized estimation, or factor the panel into a few common components and model those. This page covers the selection and factor models and the Diebold-Yilmaz spillover view that reads any closed reduced form as a network. It is not the place for small systems (Vector autoregressions) or for identification (Structural identification).

The family at a glance#

Class

Module

Model in one line

Use when

DFM

dynamic_factor

Principal components of the standardized panel, then a VAR on the factor scores (Stock-Watson two-step).

Forecasting or summarizing a wide panel driven by a few common factors.

FAVAR

factor_augmented

A VAR on factors plus observed variables, with an observation equation back to every panel series (Bernanke, Boivin and Eliasz 2005).

Responses of hundreds of series to a shock identified in a small system.

FactorSV

factor_volatility

Static factor model with stochastic volatility on every factor and idiosyncratic error; Gibbs sampler.

A time-varying covariance or correlation matrix for a panel of returns.

SparseVAR

sparse

Penalized VAR: lasso, adaptive lasso, SCAD, MCP or lag-group.

The zero pattern is the finding: a directed Granger network.

GraphicalVAR

graphical

Lasso VAR plus a sparse residual precision by nodewise regression (Barigozzi and Brownlees 2019).

You also want the contemporaneous partial-correlation network.

Spillover

spillover

Not an estimator: the generalized variance decomposition of a fitted closed reduced form as a connectedness table.

Diebold-Yilmaz total, directional and net spillovers.

BVAR, HierarchicalBVAR, GibbsBVAR, StudentBVAR, BVARSV

bayesian and siblings

Shrinkage toward a prior, with posterior draws.

See Bayesian VARs.

Choosing among them#

  • Forecast every series, few common drivers: DFM. It is one SVD and one least-squares VAR, fast at any width.

  • Structural shock, panel-wide responses: FAVAR, then FactorAugmentedSVAR.

  • Who Granger-causes whom among many series: SparseVAR; add GraphicalVAR for the within-period network.

  • Moving covariances of many returns: FactorSV.

  • Connectedness of any of the above: Spillover on a closed reduced form (VARResult, SparseVARResult, GraphicalVARResult, the factors of a DFM or FAVAR).

  • A wide VAR with bands on everything and no sparsity claim: BVAR (Bayesian VARs).

Dynamic factor model#

DFM estimates

\[x_t = \Lambda F_t + e_t, \qquad F_t = \sum_{i=1}^{p} A_i F_{t-i} + u_t,\]

on the standardized panel \(x_t = (y_t - \bar y) / s\). The first \(r\) principal components estimate the factor space (Stock and Watson 2002), and a VAR without deterministic terms is fitted to the scores. Left unspecified, \(r\) minimizes the Bai and Ng (2002) criterion

\[IC_{p2}(r) = \ln V(r) + r\,\frac{N + T}{N T}\,\ln\min(N, T).\]
import numpy as np
from cultivars.multivariate.large_dim.dynamic_factor import DFM

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n, N = 240, 40
f = np.zeros((n, 2))
for t in range(1, n):
    f[t] = np.array([[0.7, 0.1], [0.0, 0.5]]) @ f[t - 1] + rng.standard_normal(2)
loadings = rng.standard_normal((N, 2))
panel = 10.0 + f @ loadings.T + rng.standard_normal((n, N))

dfm = DFM(panel, order=1).fit()
print(dfm.n_factors, dfm.criterion_values.round(2))
print(dfm.variance_shares.round(3), dfm.factors.names)
print(dfm.loadings.shape, dfm.common_component.shape)
print(dfm.forecast(4).shape)

[0.386 0.266] ('f1', 'f2')
(40, 2) (240, 40)
(4, 40)

Specification choices#

order (required)

Lag order of the factor VAR, at least 0.

n_factors (default None)

Fix \(r\), or leave None to minimize \(IC_{p2}\) over 1 .. max_factors.

max_factors (default 8)

Upper end of the search; silently capped at min(nobs - 1, N).

series_names (default x1 ... xN)

Labels used by r_squared() and the summary.

What the result gives you#

DFMResult: factors is a full VARResult on the scores, with variables f1 ... fr (irf, fevd, forecast, identify all run there). loadings are the (N, r) orthonormal loadings on the standardized panel; means and scales undo the standardization; idiosyncratic is the standardized remainder; variance_shares the share of standardized variance each component explains; criterion_values the \(IC_{p2}\) path (empty when n_factors was fixed). The property common_component and the method forecast(steps) are in original units; r_squared() returns a {series: R^2} dict.

fit_share = dfm.r_squared()
print(len(fit_share), round(np.mean(list(fit_share.values())), 3))
print(np.allclose(dfm.common_component + dfm.idiosyncratic * dfm.scales, panel))

40 0.652
True

Factors are identified only up to rotation, so f1 and f2 carry no structural names; the common component, the R^2 values and the panel forecast are rotation invariant. The panel forecast maps the factor forecast through the loadings with the idiosyncratic part at zero, so it decays to the series means at the factor VAR’s rate.

Factor-augmented VAR#

FAVAR puts observed variables \(Y_t\) (a policy rate, say) beside the factors,

\[\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix} F_t \\ Y_t \end{pmatrix} = \sum_{i=1}^{p} A_i \begin{pmatrix} F_{t-i} \\ Y_{t-i} \end{pmatrix} + u_t, \qquad x_t = \Lambda^F F_t + \Lambda^Y Y_t + e_t .\end{split}\]

Principal components of the full panel absorb the observed block’s contemporaneous influence. Declaring the slow series (those that do not react within the period) runs the Bernanke-Boivin-Eliasz cleaning \(\hat F_t = C_t - \hat b_Y' Y_t\), which a recursive identification with the observed block ordered last needs.

from cultivars.multivariate.large_dim.factor_augmented import FAVAR
from cultivars.multivariate.structural.factor_augmented import FactorAugmentedSVAR

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 300
factor = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(1, n):
    factor[t] = 0.7 * factor[t - 1] + rng.standard_normal()
shock = rng.standard_normal(n)
rate = 0.5 * factor + shock
lam = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5, 30)
slow = np.outer(factor, lam[:15]) + 0.5 * rng.standard_normal((n, 15))
fast = np.outer(factor, lam[15:]) + 0.5 * rng.standard_normal((n, 15))
fast += 0.8 * np.outer(shock, rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5, 15))
info = np.column_stack([slow, fast])
labels = tuple(f"s{i}" for i in range(15)) + tuple(f"q{i}" for i in range(15))

favar = FAVAR(info, rate, n_factors=1, order=1, panel_names=labels,
              slow=labels[:15], observed_names=("rate",)).fit()
print(favar.cleaned, favar.factors.names, favar.loadings.shape)
print(round(float(favar.r2.mean()), 3))
svar = FactorAugmentedSVAR(favar).identify()
print(svar.shock_names, svar.irf(8).shape)

True ('f1', 'rate') (30, 2)
0.897
('f1', 'rate') (9, 30, 2)

FAVAR(panel, observed, *, n_factors, order, slow=None, trend="c", observed_names=None, panel_names=None): n_factors is required (no criterion search here), and slow needs at least n_factors + 1 names. The result carries factors (a VARResult on [F, Y]), loadings (N, r + m), scores (T, r), r2 (an array), explained_variance (before cleaning), cleaned, and the method common_component(). Identification happens afterwards: FactorAugmentedSVAR factorizes factors recursively with the observed block last by default, or maps any point-identified structural result on favar.factors, and returns responses of all \(N\) panel series; see Structural identification.

Factor stochastic volatility#

FactorSV models a demeaned panel as

\[y_t = \Lambda f_t + \varepsilon_t, \quad f_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, H_t), \quad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, D_t), \qquad h_{j,t+1} = \mu_j + \phi_j (h_{jt} - \mu_j) + \sigma_j \xi_{jt},\]

with a log-AR(1) variance on each of the \(r\) factors and \(k\) idiosyncratic errors, so \(\Sigma_t = \Lambda H_t \Lambda' + D_t\) moves through \(k + r\) paths (Pitt and Shephard 1999; Chib, Nardari and Shephard 2006). Estimation is a Gibbs sampler with Kim-Shephard-Chib mixture blocks. \(\Lambda\) is lower triangular with a unit diagonal in its first \(r\) rows, so the first n_factors columns anchor the factors’ scale and sign.

from cultivars.multivariate.large_dim.factor_volatility import FactorSV

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
n = 200
h = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(1, n):
    h[t] = 0.95 * h[t - 1] + 0.3 * rng.standard_normal()
common = np.exp(h / 2) * rng.standard_normal(n)
returns = np.outer(common, [1.0, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4]) + 0.5 * rng.standard_normal((n, 4))

fsv = FactorSV(returns, n_factors=1).fit(n_draws=300, n_burn=100, seed=0)
print(fsv.n_kept, fsv.loadings[:, 0].round(2))
print(fsv.communality().round(2))
print(fsv.factor_volatility(0).shape, fsv.conditional_correlation().shape)

200 [1.   0.84 0.63 0.45]
[0.86 0.71 0.62 0.45]
(3, 200) (200, 4, 4)

FactorSV(panel, *, n_factors, series_names=None) requires at least 50 rows and n_factors < k. fit(n_draws=3000, n_burn=1000, thin=1, prior=None, loading_prior_precision=1.0, seed=None) counts n_draws including burn-in; prior is a VolatilityPrior for the log-variance laws. The result stores loading_draws, factor_draws, h_factor_draws, h_idio_draws and the AR parameters’ draws; loadings and factors are posterior-mean properties; factor_volatility(j) and idiosyncratic_volatility(series) return (len(quantiles), T) quantiles of the standard deviation; conditional_covariance() and conditional_correlation() are (T, k, k) posterior means; communality() is the time-averaged factor share per series. The sampler does not use ancillarity-sufficiency interweaving (Kastner, Fruehwirth-Schnatter and Lopes 2017), so loadings and volatility paths mix slowly; read convergence() before the point summaries.

Sparse VAR#

SparseVAR solves, on the standardized panel,

\[\hat A = \arg\min_A\ \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_t \Bigl\lVert y_t - c - \sum_{i=1}^{p} A_i y_{t-i} \Bigr\rVert^2 + \lambda\, P(A),\]

with \(P\) the lasso (Basu and Michailidis 2015), the adaptive lasso (ridge-pilot weights), SCAD or MCP (local linear approximation, Zou and Li 2008), or the lag-group penalty \(\sum_l \sqrt{k^2}\,\lVert A_l \rVert_F\) that keeps or drops whole lag matrices (Nicholson, Matteson and Bien 2017). Deterministic terms are never penalized. When lam is not given it is chosen along a geometric path by rolling-origin one-step forecast cross-validation with a refit at every origin.

from cultivars.multivariate.large_dim.sparse import SparseVAR

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
k, n = 8, 300
A = 0.4 * np.eye(k)
A[1, 0], A[2, 0], A[5, 4] = 0.3, 0.3, -0.3
y = np.zeros((n, k))
for t in range(1, n):
    y[t] = A @ y[t - 1] + rng.standard_normal(k)

lasso = SparseVAR(y, order=1).fit(penalty="lasso")
print(round(lasso.lam, 4), lasso.n_nonzero, round(lasso.sparsity, 3))
print(lasso.lambda_path.shape, lasso.cv_errors.shape)
edges = lasso.granger_adjacency(threshold=0.1)
print([(i, j) for i in range(k) for j in range(k) if edges[i, j]])

0.0357 35 0.453
(20,) (20,)
[(1, 0), (2, 0), (5, 4)]

granger_adjacency(threshold) returns a (k, k) boolean matrix with [i, j] true when some lag of variable j enters equation i above the threshold; the diagonal is always false. At the default threshold=0.0 every surviving coefficient is an edge, and the forecast-optimal penalty keeps many small ones.

fit(penalty="lasso", lam=None, n_lambdas=20, lambda_min_ratio=1e-3); penalty is one of "lasso", "adaptive", "scad", "mcp", "group". The result has coefficients (p, k, k), deterministic, sigma_u (residual scatter over nobs minus the average active regressors per equation), resid, fittedvalues, penalty, lam (on the standardized scale), lambda_path and cv_errors (both empty when lam was given), n_nonzero, sparsity and ma_representation(horizon). It reports no likelihood, information criteria or standard errors: post-selection inference is not attempted.

grouped = SparseVAR(y, order=2).fit(penalty="group", n_lambdas=10)
print(round(grouped.lam, 4), [int(np.count_nonzero(b)) for b in grouped.coefficients])
stated = SparseVAR(y, order=2).fit(penalty="group", lam=0.05)
print([int(np.count_nonzero(b)) for b in stated.coefficients])

0.0455 [64, 64]
[64, 0]

Each lag block is either entirely zero or entirely free. The cross-validated level (0.0455) keeps the second lag although the simulated system has only one; a slightly larger stated level, 0.05, drops it.

Graphical VAR#

GraphicalVAR adds the second network. Stage one is the lasso VAR above; stage two estimates the precision of the residuals by Meinshausen-Buhlmann nodewise lasso regressions, each with its own penalty chosen by K-fold cross-validation, symmetrized by averaging. A zero in the precision is a zero partial correlation: conditional independence of two innovations given the others.

from cultivars.multivariate.large_dim.graphical import GraphicalVAR

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
k, n = 5, 400
theta = np.eye(k)
theta[0, 1] = theta[1, 0] = -0.5
theta[2, 3] = theta[3, 2] = 0.5
chol = np.linalg.cholesky(np.linalg.inv(theta))
coef = 0.5 * np.eye(k)
coef[1, 0] = coef[2, 1] = 0.4
z = np.zeros((n, k))
for t in range(1, n):
    z[t] = coef @ z[t - 1] + chol @ rng.standard_normal(k)

graph = GraphicalVAR(z, order=1).fit(seed=0)
within = graph.contemporaneous_adjacency(threshold=0.3)
print([(i, j) for i in range(k) for j in range(i + 1, k) if within[i, j]])
across = graph.granger_adjacency(threshold=0.2)
print([(i, j) for i in range(k) for j in range(k) if across[i, j]])
print(graph.partial_correlations.round(2)[0, 1], graph.lam_nodes.shape)

[(0, 1), (2, 3)]
[(1, 0), (2, 1)]
0.47 (5,)

fit(lam=None, n_lambdas=15, lambda_min_ratio=1e-3, n_folds=5, seed=None); the first stage is always the lasso (there is no penalty argument), and seed drives the fold shuffle of the second stage. The result is a SparseVARResult plus precision, partial_correlations, lam_nodes and contemporaneous_adjacency(threshold=0.05). Neither network is a causal graph or a test.

Spillover tables#

Spillover reads the generalized forecast-error variance decomposition (Koop, Pesaran and Potter 1996; Pesaran and Shin 1998) of a fitted closed reduced form as a directed network (Diebold and Yilmaz 2009, 2012):

\[\theta_{ij}(H) = \frac{\sigma_{jj}^{-1} \sum_{h=0}^{H} (e_i' \Psi_h \Sigma_u e_j)^2} {\sum_{h=0}^{H} e_i' \Psi_h \Sigma_u \Psi_h' e_i}, \qquad \tilde\theta_{ij}(H) = 100\,\frac{\theta_{ij}(H)}{\sum_j \theta_{ij}(H)} .\]

It needs only names, k_endog, sigma_u and ma_representation, so it takes a VARResult (shrunk or not), a SparseVARResult, a GraphicalVARResult, or the factors of a DFM or FAVAR. Running it on the lasso VAR is the Demirer, Diebold, Liu and Yilmaz (2018) pipeline:

from cultivars.multivariate.large_dim.spillover import Spillover

table = Spillover(lasso, horizon=10).compute()
print(round(table.total, 2), table.transmitters())
print(table.table.shape, np.allclose(table.table.sum(axis=1), 100.0))
print(table.net.round(1))
print(round(Spillover(dfm.factors, horizon=10).compute().total, 2))

6.26 ('y1', 'y5', 'y8')
(8, 8) True
[ 22.6 -13.6 -10.5  -1.2   9.4  -5.2  -1.5   0.1]
1.13

Spillover(result, *, horizon=10) then compute(). horizon is the largest moving-average lead, so \(H + 1\) terms are summed and the table equals 100 * result.generalized_fevd(horizon)[-1] on a VARResult. SpilloverResult has table (rows sum to 100; entry [i, j] is the share of i’s variance due to shocks to j), total (average off-diagonal mass, in percent), to_others (column sums), from_others (row sums), net, net_pairwise and transmitters() (names with positive net, largest first). Because generalized shocks are correlated the table is a relative attribution, not an additive decomposition. For the rolling-window picture, refit on each window and call Spillover again; there is no built-in rolling helper.

How this relates to the Bayesian VARs#

The three answers differ in what they claim and what they return.

Select (SparseVAR, GraphicalVAR)

Factor (DFM, FAVAR, FactorSV)

Coefficients are near a prior mean.

Most coefficients are exactly zero.

A few factors drive the comovement.

Uncertainty

Posterior draws, 68% bands.

None reported.

Two-step: none for the factor step; FactorSV is a posterior.

Closed system for Spillover / identification

No (except VAR(prior=...)).

Yes.

Yes, through .factors (DFM, FAVAR).

A BVAR result propagates through its own draws and does not satisfy the closed-system protocol, so Spillover refuses it. For a shrunk network, fit the point-estimate path with the same prior:

from cultivars.bayes.priors import NormalInverseWishartPrior
from cultivars.exceptions import SpecificationError
from cultivars.multivariate.large_dim.bayesian import BVAR
from cultivars.multivariate.reduced_form.vector_autoregression import VAR

prior = NormalInverseWishartPrior(persistence=0.0)
posterior = BVAR(y, order=1, prior=prior).fit(n_draws=50, seed=0)
try:
    Spillover(posterior)
except SpecificationError as err:
    print(str(err)[:60])
shrunk = VAR(y, order=1, prior=prior).fit()
print(np.allclose(shrunk.coefficients, posterior.coefficients))
print(round(Spillover(shrunk, horizon=10).compute().total, 2))

How the pieces connect#

  • Factor VARs are VARs. DFMResult.factors and FAVARResult.factors are ordinary VARResult objects: forecast, irf, fevd, Granger tests, and every identification model on Structural identification apply. FactorAugmentedSVAR maps the identified factor-space shocks onto all panel series.

  • Closed-system contract. SparseVARResult and GraphicalVARResult expose names, k_endog, sigma_u and ma_representation, which is what Spillover and the structural layer read.

  • Bayesian tools. FactorSVResult.convergence() is the same diagnostic as on the Bayesian VARs (The Bayesian workflow). The penalized and two-step factor models have no posterior and no marginal likelihood.

  • Forecasting. DFMResult.forecast gives point paths for the whole panel; the factor VAR’s forecast gives the factor paths. For predictive densities on a wide system use BVAR.forecast_paths (Bayesian VARs, Forecasting and forecast evaluation).

Nuances and gotchas#

  • SparseVAR and GraphicalVAR have no forecast or irf. Their results offer only ma_representation among the propagation methods; build forecasts from coefficients and deterministic, or impulse responses from ma_representation and sigma_u.

  • FAVAR’s observation equation has no intercept. loadings come from regressing the standardized (mean-zero) panel on [F, Y] without a constant, while Y is used as supplied. Shifting the observed block by a constant changes loadings, r2 and common_component(). Demean the observed variables before passing them if you read the observation equation.

  • Inconsistent accessors across the factor results. DFMResult.common_component is a property and r_squared() a method returning a dict; FAVARResult.common_component() is a method and r2 an array attribute. The panel labels are series_names on DFM and FactorSV and panel_names on FAVAR.

  • Factor labels are a basis. In DFM and FAVAR the factors are principal components, identified up to rotation; in FactorSV they are pinned by the triangular convention, so reordering the columns of the panel changes the posterior.

  • The Bai-Ng count can overselect when the common component explains nearly all of the panel; fix n_factors if the selected count looks generous next to variance_shares.

  • Selected support is not inference. Edges depend on lam and on threshold. The cross-validated level is prediction-optimal and keeps more than the true support; the nonconvex penalties are designed for support recovery, but on any one sample they can keep spurious links too. Inspect lambda_path and cv_errors.

  • lam is not comparable across penalties. It is on the standardized scale and each penalty’s path starts from its own zeroing level.

  • Spillover horizon counts from zero. horizon=10 sums \(\Psi_0, \ldots, \Psi_{10}\), eleven terms.

  • Run time. SparseVAR refits at every rolling origin for every path point. With defaults, the 8-variable example above takes about 0.2 s at one lag and about 1.5 s at two lags; penalty="mcp" roughly doubles that. FactorSV at 300 iterations on four series takes a few seconds; real applications need thousands.
