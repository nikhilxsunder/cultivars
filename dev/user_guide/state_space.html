State-space models and filtering#

The cultivars.state_space package holds the four filtering engines that every latent-state model in the library runs on: a linear-Gaussian system, a nonlinear system, a discrete Markov chain over an autoregression, and a linear system whose matrices switch with such a chain. An engine is a fully specified system that filters, smooths and evaluates a likelihood; it has no parameters, no fit and no estimator. Use this page to write a system down directly, or to understand and re-use the engine behind a fitted unobserved-components, term-structure, volatility or regime-switching model. Estimating those models is covered on their own pages (Univariate linear models, Volatility models, Nonlinear and regime-switching univariate models, Beyond the basic VAR, Nonlinear, time-varying and regime-switching VARs).

One grammar for four engines#

The engines share one workflow: construct a fully specified system, hand it data, and read a record. LinearGaussianSSM, MarkovSwitchingSSM and RegimeSwitchingLinearSSM expose the same five members: k_endog (observed dimension), k_states, filter(y), smooth(y) and loglikelihood(y). NonlinearSSM has k_endog and k_states but no single filter: its three filters return different kinds of likelihood, so you choose one by name (extended_filter, unscented_filter, particle_filter).

Three rules hold across the package:

  • Data layout. Observations are (n, p) with time down the rows; a 1-D array is accepted when p is one. Every state array in a result has time on the first axis: states are (n, m), covariances (n, m, m), regime probabilities (n, K).

  • Missing data are numpy.nan. A missing period contributes exactly zero to the log-likelihood and the filter carries its prediction forward. The linear engine handles element-wise gaps; the nonlinear and Kim engines accept only whole missing rows; the Hamilton engine accepts none (see Nuances and gotchas).

  • Every likelihood says what it is. The Kalman and Hamilton likelihoods are exact; the extended and unscented ones are exact likelihoods of an approximating model; the particle likelihood is an unbiased Monte Carlo estimate; the Kim likelihood is an approximation. loglikelihood is the sum of loglikelihood_contributions, the per-period prediction-error terms \(\log p(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1})\).

Linear-Gaussian systems#

The model#

LinearGaussianSSM uses the notation of Durbin and Koopman (2012):

\[ \begin{align}\begin{aligned}y_t = Z_t \alpha_t + d_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim N(0, H_t),\\\alpha_{t+1} = T_t \alpha_t + c_t + R_t \eta_t, \qquad \eta_t \sim N(0, Q_t), \qquad \alpha_1 \sim N(a_1, P_1).\end{aligned}\end{align} \]

The Kalman filter returns the prediction-error decomposition

\[\log L = -\tfrac12 \sum_{t=1}^{n} \bigl(p_t \log 2\pi + \log|F_t| + v_t' F_t^{-1} v_t\bigr), \qquad v_t = y_t - Z_t a_t - d_t,\quad F_t = Z_t P_t Z_t' + H_t,\]

with \(p_t\) the number of elements observed at \(t\). The smoother is the Durbin-Koopman backward recursion, and the simulation smoother draws \(\alpha \mid y\) by the mean-correction device of Durbin and Koopman (2002).

A local level, end to end#

Initialization#

When initial_state_cov is omitted the engine picks \(P_1\) by a stated rule:

  • If transition, selection and state_cov are all time-invariant and every eigenvalue of \(T\) is inside the unit circle, \(P_1\) is the stationary covariance solving \(P = TPT' + RQR'\). The classmethod stationary_covariance() solves the same Lyapunov equation on its own and raises NumericalError for an unstable \(T\).

  • Otherwise \(P_1 = 10^6 I\): the approximate diffuse prior, not the exact diffuse initialization of Durbin and Koopman (2012, ch. 5).

Above, the random-walk level got the diffuse prior; its first contribution is about \(-\tfrac12(\log 2\pi + \log 10^6)\). An AR(1) state gets the stationary variance \(0.5 / (1 - 0.8^2) = 1.3889\):

print(float(filtered.predicted_state_cov[0, 0, 0]),
      round(float(filtered.loglikelihood_contributions[0]), 3))
ar1 = LinearGaussianSSM([[1.0]], [[1.0]], [[0.8]], [[1.0]], [[0.5]])
print(ar1.filter(y).predicted_state_cov[0].round(4).tolist())
print(LinearGaussianSSM.stationary_covariance(
    [[0.8]], [[1.0]], [[0.5]]).round(4).tolist())

1000000.0 -7.827
[[1.3889]]
[[1.3889]]

The initial mean has no such rule: initial_state defaults to zero even when a state_intercept moves the stationary mean to \((I - T)^{-1} c\). Pass it yourself:

shifted = LinearGaussianSSM(
    [[1.0]], [[1.0]], [[0.8]], [[1.0]], [[0.5]], state_intercept=[1.0],
)
print(shifted.filter(y).predicted_state[0].tolist())
centred = LinearGaussianSSM(
    [[1.0]], [[1.0]], [[0.8]], [[1.0]], [[0.5]], state_intercept=[1.0],
    initial_state=[5.0],
)

What the records give you#

filter returns KalmanFilterResult. Row t of predicted_state is \(a_{t \mid t-1}\) (row 0 is \(a_1\)), row t of filtered_state is \(a_{t \mid t}\), and the covariances follow the same indexing. smooth returns DurbinKoopmanSmootherResult with \(\hat\alpha_{t \mid n}\) and its variance. loglikelihood(y) is the likelihood-only path, the call an optimizer should make.

Nonlinear and non-Gaussian systems#

The model#

NonlinearSSM holds the additive-Gaussian system

\[y_t = g(\alpha_t) + \varepsilon_t,\qquad \varepsilon_t \sim N(0, H), \qquad \alpha_{t+1} = f(\alpha_t) + \eta_t,\qquad \eta_t \sim N(0, Q), \qquad \alpha_1 \sim N(a_1, P_1).\]

There is no selection matrix: state_cov is the (m, m) covariance of the full state disturbance. The two maps are positional and batched: transition maps an (N, m) block of states to (N, m) and observation maps it to (N, p), so sigma points and particle clouds pass through them in one call. The constructor probes both maps with a two-row batch and raises DimensionError if the shapes are wrong; p is read from obs_cov. initial_state and initial_state_cov are required: there is no default initialization.

Non-Gaussian models through hooks#

Three keyword hooks take a model outside the additive-Gaussian form:

observation_loglik(obs, particles)

Replaces the Gaussian measurement density: obs is one (p,) row, particles is (N, m), and it returns (N,) log-densities. obs_cov is then ignored by the particle filter.

transition_sampler(particles, rng)

Replaces the additive state draw; returns (N, m).

observed_transition(particles, y_prev)

A state mean that conditions on the previous observation; noise stays \(N(0, Q)\).

With any hook set, is_additive_gaussian is False and the extended and unscented filters and smoothers raise SpecificationError instead of linearizing. The particle filter accepts all three hooks. The particle smoother accepts observation_loglik but refuses transition_sampler, because the backward pass needs the transition density, and refuses a singular state_cov for the same reason. A stochastic-volatility measurement, \(y_t \mid h_t \sim N(0, e^{h_t})\):

def measure(obs, particles):
    h = particles[:, 0]
    return -0.5 * (np.log(2 * np.pi) + h + obs[0] ** 2 * np.exp(-h))

returns = np.exp(0.5 * state) * rng.standard_normal(n)
sv = NonlinearSSM(
    lambda s: 0.9 * s, lambda s: s,
    state_cov=[[0.25]], obs_cov=[[1.0]],
    initial_state=[0.0], initial_state_cov=[[0.25 / 0.19]],
    observation_loglik=measure,
)
print(sv.is_additive_gaussian)
try:
    sv.unscented_filter(returns)
except Exception as err:
    print(type(err).__name__)
track = sv.particle_filter(returns, n_particles=500, seed=0)
print(round(float(np.corrcoef(track.filtered_state[:, 0], state)[0, 1]), 2))

Discrete regimes: the Hamilton filter#

The model#

MarkovSwitchingSSM is an autoregression whose intercept and variance switch with a \(K\)-state chain \(S_t\),

\[y_t = \mu_{S_t} + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_{i,S_t}\, y_{t-i} + \sigma_{S_t} e_t, \qquad P_{ij} = \Pr(S_t = j \mid S_{t-1} = i).\]

Because the regime enters only through the current intercept and variance, the per-regime density \(\eta_{t,j}\) depends on \(S_t\) alone and the Hamilton (1989) filter

\[\xi_{t \mid t-1} = P'\,\xi_{t-1 \mid t-1},\qquad \xi_{t \mid t} = \frac{\xi_{t \mid t-1} \odot \eta_t} {\mathbf 1'(\xi_{t \mid t-1} \odot \eta_t)},\qquad \log L = \sum_t \log \mathbf 1'(\xi_{t \mid t-1} \odot \eta_t)\]

is exact. The Kim smoother returns full-sample probabilities and the consecutive-pair probabilities an EM step needs.

from cultivars.state_space.regime_switching import (
    MarkovSwitchingSSM, RegimeSwitchingLinearSSM,
)

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
chain = np.array([[0.95, 0.05], [0.10, 0.90]])
n = 300
regime = np.zeros(n, dtype=int)
z = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(1, n):
    regime[t] = rng.choice(2, p=chain[regime[t - 1]])

truth = regime[ms.order:] == 1

      round(float(np.mean((kim.smoothed_prob[:, 1] > 0.5) == truth)), 2))

The probability arrays have n - order rows: the first order observations are conditioned on, not modelled. Row t of filtered_prob belongs to observation t + order.

The recursions never see the data, only an (n - order, K) matrix of log densities. effective_sample, density_matrix, filter_densities and smooth_densities expose that split, so an EM loop can build the lag block once, and a switching model of another form can supply its own log densities:

target, lags = ms.effective_sample(z)
logd = ms.density_matrix(target, lags)
print(logd.shape,
      bool(np.isclose(ms.filter_densities(logd).loglikelihood,
                      hamilton.loglikelihood)))

Switching linear systems: Kim’s collapse#

RegimeSwitchingLinearSSM gives each regime its own linear-Gaussian system,

\[y_t = Z_j \alpha_t + d_j + \varepsilon_t,\quad \varepsilon_t \sim N(0, H_j), \qquad \alpha_{t+1} = T_j \alpha_t + c_j + \eta_t,\quad \eta_t \sim N(0, Q_j), \qquad j = S_t.\]

Exact filtering would need one Kalman filter per regime path, so the engine applies Kim’s (1994) collapse: \(K \times K\) pairwise updates, weighted by the Hamilton probabilities and moment-matched back to one mean and covariance per regime. The likelihood is therefore an approximation (Kim and Nelson 1999). All arguments are keyword-only and stacked on a leading regime axis: design (K, p, m), obs_cov (K, p, p), transition (K, m, m), state_cov (K, m, m) (no selection matrix), regime_transition (K, K), and optional obs_intercept (K, p), state_intercept (K, m), initial_state (m,), initial_state_cov (m, m) and initial_regime_prob (K,).

A state whose innovation variance switches between a calm and a volatile regime:

x = np.zeros(n)
calm_or_not = np.zeros(n, dtype=int)
for t in range(1, n):
    calm_or_not[t] = rng.choice(2, p=chain[calm_or_not[t - 1]])
    scale = np.sqrt([0.05, 1.0][calm_or_not[t]])
    x[t] = 0.9 * x[t - 1] + scale * rng.standard_normal()
obs = x + np.sqrt(0.5) * rng.standard_normal(n)

switching = RegimeSwitchingLinearSSM(
    design=np.ones((2, 1, 1)), obs_cov=np.full((2, 1, 1), 0.5),
    transition=np.full((2, 1, 1), 0.9),
    state_cov=np.array([[[0.05]], [[1.0]]]),
    regime_transition=chain,
)
print(switching.k_regimes, switching.k_states, switching.k_endog)
collapsed = switching.filter(obs)
print(collapsed.filtered_prob.shape, collapsed.filtered_state.shape,
      collapsed.regime_state.shape)
print(round(collapsed.loglikelihood, 2))
print(switching.smooth(obs).smoothed_prob.shape)

2 1 1
(300, 2) (300, 1) (300, 2, 1)
-374.8
(300, 2)

filtered_state and filtered_state_cov are regime-marginal (the covariance includes the between-regime spread); regime_state holds each regime’s collapsed mean. Unlike the Hamilton engine, the Kim filter keeps all n rows. smooth smooths the regime probabilities only: a smoothed continuous state would need a second collapse and is not offered.

How the pieces connect#

      bool(abs(system.loglikelihood(series) - uc.llf) < 1e-8))

Call an engine directly when the system is yours: a model the library does not estimate, a likelihood you will maximize with your own optimizer or sampler (build the engine inside the objective and call loglikelihood), a fitted system evaluated on a new sample, or a stress test of a fitted system under modified matrices.

Nuances and gotchas#

  • Result records are plain dataclasses. None of the eight records has summary(), to_pandas() or the comparison methods of fitted results; their repr is the default dataclass repr, which prints every array in full (over 20,000 characters for the 200-period local level) under the private class name:

    print(hasattr(filtered, "summary"), len(repr(filtered)) > 20000)
print(type(filtered).__name__)

    False True
_KalmanFilterResult

    Read the fields by name instead of printing the record.

  • The diffuse prior is approximate. \(P_1 = 10^6 I\) puts about \(-\tfrac12\log 10^6 \approx -6.9\) into the first contributions for each nonstationary state. Likelihoods are comparable across specifications with the same number of diffuse states on the same sample, not across different numbers of them. A time-varying transition, selection or state_cov also triggers the diffuse default even when every \(T_t\) is stable; pass initial_state_cov explicitly in that case.

  • The default initial mean is zero. See Initialization: with a state_intercept, set initial_state to the stationary mean.

  • Missing-data rules differ by engine. LinearGaussianSSM accepts nan in any element. NonlinearSSM and RegimeSwitchingLinearSSM accept only whole missing rows and raise SpecificationError on a partially observed row. MarkovSwitchingSSM.filter raises on any non-finite value, because the lags of a missing value poison later densities; interpolate first or use RegimeSwitchingLinearSSM.

  • Row counts differ. Hamilton-filter arrays have n - order rows; Kalman, Kim and particle arrays have n. Align by index before comparing a regime path with anything dated on the full sample, such as a TurningPointsResult contraction indicator.

  • Default initializations differ between the linear engines. RegimeSwitchingLinearSSM defaults to \(P_1 = 100\, I\), not the stationary covariance, so two identical regimes do not reproduce LinearGaussianSSM until you pass the same initial_state_cov:

    same = dict(
    design=np.ones((2, 1, 1)), obs_cov=np.ones((2, 1, 1)),
    transition=np.full((2, 1, 1), 0.8), state_cov=np.full((2, 1, 1), 0.5),
    regime_transition=chain,
)
kalman = LinearGaussianSSM([[1.0]], [[1.0]], [[0.8]], [[1.0]], [[0.5]])
exact = kalman.loglikelihood(obs)
print(round(RegimeSwitchingLinearSSM(**same).loglikelihood(obs) - exact, 3))
matched = RegimeSwitchingLinearSSM(**same, initial_state_cov=[[0.5 / 0.36]])
print(bool(abs(matched.loglikelihood(obs) - exact) < 1e-8))

    -1.919
True

  • A bad chain raises different exceptions. A transition matrix that is not row-stochastic raises SpecificationError in MarkovSwitchingSSM and NumericalError in RegimeSwitchingLinearSSM. Catch both if you validate user input.

  • The two nonlinear notations. The NonlinearSSM docstrings call the measurement covariance \(R\); on this page it is \(H\), to match the linear engine, where \(R\) is the selection matrix. The argument names (obs_cov, state_cov) are the same in both engines.

  • Unscented tuning must match. unscented_smoother reruns the filter with its own alpha, beta and kappa; pass the same values you gave unscented_filter or the two will not correspond.

  • Particle results do not keep the cloud. ParticleFilterResult carries cloud means and standard deviations, not particles or weights. particle_smoother costs \(O(N^2)\) per period and stores every forward cloud, which is why its default n_particles is 500 rather than 2000.

  • Seeds. particle_filter, particle_smoother, simulate and simulation_smoother take seed (an int or a numpy.random.Generator); with seed=None every call differs.

  • No forecasting method. The engines have no forecast. Append nan rows to the data and read predicted_state from the filter, or use the forecast of the fitted model that emitted the engine.

  • The engines do not estimate. There is no fit and no parameter vector. To estimate a custom system, build the engine inside your objective function and maximize loglikelihood.
