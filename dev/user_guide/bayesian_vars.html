Bayesian VARs#

A Bayesian VAR replaces the least-squares estimate of a vector autoregression with a posterior under a prior that shrinks the coefficients toward a stated belief, usually independent random walks. Shrinkage is what makes a system with more regressors than observations estimable, and the posterior draws give credible bands for every propagated object: coefficients, impulse responses and the predictive distribution. This page covers the prior objects in priors, the five Bayesian VAR estimators in cultivars.multivariate.large_dim, and the prior= argument of the point-estimate VAR. Penalized and factor models for wide panels are on Large and high-dimensional systems; the unrestricted VAR is on Vector autoregressions; marginal likelihoods, predictive checks and model averaging in general are on The Bayesian workflow.

All examples on this page share one simulated system: three stationary variables with non-zero means and three cross-variable links.

import numpy as np
from cultivars.multivariate.large_dim.bayesian import BVAR

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
A = np.array([[0.6, 0.2, 0.0],
              [0.0, 0.5, 0.1],
              [0.1, 0.0, 0.4]])
y = np.zeros((200, 3))
for t in range(1, 200):
    y[t] = A @ y[t - 1] + rng.standard_normal(3)
y += np.array([2.0, 3.0, 4.0])
names = ("output", "prices", "rate")

Priors are objects#

A prior is a frozen dataclass from priors. It states three things about the (w, k) coefficient matrix \(B\) (rows in design order: deterministic terms, then lag blocks; columns are equations): its mean (coefficient_mean), its variance (coefficient_variance), and any pseudo-observations stacked under the sample (dummy_observations). Each hook takes a PriorContext describing the sample, so a prior knows nothing about the estimator that reads it.

The Minnesota variance of the coefficient on variable \(j\) at lag \(l\) in equation \(i\), with \(s_i\) the residual scale of a univariate autoregression of variable \(i\), is (Litterman 1986)

\[\begin{split}\operatorname{Var}(A_{l,ij}) = \Bigl(\frac{\lambda_1}{l^{\lambda_3}}\Bigr)^{2} \times \begin{cases} 1 & i = j \\ \lambda_2^2\, s_i^2 / s_j^2 & i \ne j \end{cases}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(c_i) = (\lambda_1 \lambda_4 s_i)^2 .\end{split}\]

The conjugate BVAR#

BVAR estimates

\[y_t = c + \sum_{l=1}^{p} A_l\, y_{t-l} + u_t, \qquad u_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma),\]

stacked as \(Y = XB + U\), under

\[\Sigma \sim \mathcal{IW}(S_0, k + 2), \qquad \operatorname{vec}(B) \mid \Sigma \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\operatorname{vec}(B_0),\ \Sigma \otimes \Omega\bigr),\]

with \(S_0 = \operatorname{diag}(s_1^2, \ldots, s_k^2)\). The Kronecker form makes the posterior Normal-inverse-Wishart in closed form (Kadiyala and Karlsson 1997; Banbura, Giannone and Reichlin 2010): \(K = \Omega^{-1} + X^\top X\), \(\bar B = K^{-1}(\Omega^{-1} B_0 + X^\top Y)\), and the draws are independent samples, not a Markov chain.

res = BVAR(y, order=2, names=names).fit(n_draws=500, seed=0)
print(res.nobs, res.n_kept, res.n_dummy, res.posterior_df)
print(res.beta_draws.shape, res.sigma_draws.shape, res.coefficients.shape)
print(res.credible_interval("output", "prices.L1").round(3))

Specification choices#

order (required)

Positive integer; order=0 is refused because the Minnesota prior centres the own first lag.

prior (default NormalInverseWishartPrior())

Any conjugacy-compatible prior or composition; see the table above.

trend (default "c")

"n", "c" or "ct". The deterministic block gets standard deviation \(\lambda_1 \lambda_4 s_i\), effectively unshrunk.

names (default y1 ... yk)

Also the recursive ordering of every orthogonalized impulse response.

fit(n_draws=1000, seed=None)

n_draws independent draws, all kept (no burn-in, no thinning). The posterior mean, sigma_u and log_marginal_likelihood do not depend on n_draws or seed; only Monte Carlo bands do.

What the result gives you#

BVARResult carries beta_mean ((w, k), exact \(\bar B\)), coefficients ((p, k, k), coefficients[l-1][i, j] is the effect of variable j at lag l on equation i), deterministic, sigma_u (exact posterior mean \(\bar S / (\bar\nu - k - 1)\)), beta_draws, sigma_draws, resid and fittedvalues at the posterior mean, nobs (\(T - p\), dummy rows excluded), n_dummy, posterior_df, log_marginal_likelihood, prior_label and stable_share (share of draws with companion spectral radius below one). Methods: credible_interval, irf, irf_bands, forecast, forecast_paths, marginal_likelihood, convergence, simulate, posterior_replications and summary. There is no llf, aic or n_params.

Impulse responses with bands#

irf(horizon=20, orthogonalized=True, cumulative=False) is the posterior mean of the responses computed draw by draw, each draw rotated by its own Cholesky factor; irf_bands adds the 16th and 84th percentiles on a last axis. Entry [h, i, j] is the response of variable i at lead h to shock j.

bands = res.irf_bands(12)
print(bands.shape)
print(bands[[0, 1, 4], 0, 1].round(3))   # output's response to a prices shock

(13, 3, 3, 3)
[[0.    0.    0.   ]
 [0.029 0.089 0.151]
 [0.026 0.092 0.156]]

Rows are leads 0, 1 and 4; columns are (16th, mean, 84th). The impact response is exactly zero because output is ordered before prices: the recursive ordering of names is an identifying assumption. The posterior mean response is not the response at the posterior mean coefficients beyond lead one, because \(\Psi_h\) is a polynomial of degree \(h\) in the lag matrices.

Forecasts and the predictive#

forecast_paths(steps, seed=) simulates one future per draw, each under its own \((B, \Sigma)\) and shocks, so the paths sample the joint posterior predictive. forecast is their pointwise (16th, mean, 84th) summary, shape (steps, k, 3).

paths = res.forecast_paths(4, seed=1)
fc = res.forecast(4, seed=1)
print(paths.shape, fc.shape)
print(fc[:, 0].round(2))
# a path-dependent probability: output stays below 2 for four quarters
print(round(float((paths[:, :, 0] < 2.0).all(axis=1).mean()), 3))

Marginal likelihood and prior choice#

log_marginal_likelihood is \(\log p(Y)\) in closed form, with any dummy-observation content divided out, \(\log p(Y \mid Y_d) = \log p(Y, Y_d) - \log p(Y_d)\), so priors with and without dummies are comparable. Differences across priors on the same sample are log Bayes factors (Giannone, Lenza and Primiceri 2015). marginal_likelihood() wraps the number in the record that compare() ranks:

from cultivars.bayes.evidence import compare

candidates = {
    "tight": NormalInverseWishartPrior(tightness=0.05),
    "default": NormalInverseWishartPrior(),
    "loose": NormalInverseWishartPrior(tightness=1.0),
    "stationary": NormalInverseWishartPrior(persistence=0.0),
    "sims-zha": NormalInverseWishartPrior() + SumOfCoefficientsPrior()
                + DummyInitialObservationPrior(),
}
records = [
    BVAR(y, order=2, names=names, prior=p).fit(n_draws=10, seed=0).marginal_likelihood()
    for p in candidates.values()
]
print(records[1].method, records[1].mcse, records[1].source)
table = compare(*records, names=tuple(candidates))
for row in table.rows:
    print(row[0], row[1], row[3], row[5])

Comparing lag orders#

A fit of order \(p\) scores \(T - p\) observations, so marginal likelihoods of different orders are not Bayes factors as fitted; compare adds a note to the table rather than refusing. Trim the sample so every order conditions on the same presample:

p_max = 4
by_order = [
    BVAR(y[p_max - p:], order=p).fit(n_draws=10, seed=0).marginal_likelihood()
    for p in range(1, p_max + 1)
]
print({r.nobs for r in by_order})
print(compare(*by_order).rows[0][0])

BVAR(1), niw(l1=0.2, l3=1, l4=100)

Refused priors#

The conjugate model checks that the prior variance factors as \(\sigma_i^2 \omega_j\) and refuses a prior that does not, rather than pinning \(\lambda_2\) itself:

from cultivars.exceptions import SpecificationError

try:
    BVAR(y, order=2, prior=MinnesotaPrior()).fit(n_draws=10)
except SpecificationError as err:
    print(str(err)[:68])

the prior variance does not factor as sigma_i^2 * omega_col, so Norm

Use GibbsBVAR for a sampled posterior under that prior, or VAR(..., prior=MinnesotaPrior()) for the shrunk point estimate.

Estimated tightness: HierarchicalBVAR#

HierarchicalBVAR treats \(\theta = (\lambda, \mu, \delta)\) (overall tightness, sum-of-coefficients and dummy-initial-observation looseness, larger is looser, as in Giannone, Lenza and Primiceri 2015) as unknowns with Gamma hyperpriors (mode 0.2, sd 0.4 for \(\lambda\); mode 1, sd 1 for \(\mu, \delta\)), and uses the conjugate marginal likelihood as their likelihood:

\[p(\theta \mid y) \propto p(y \mid \theta)\, p(\theta).\]

method="empirical" conditions on the mode; method="full" (the default) runs a random-walk Metropolis chain over \(\log\theta\) and draws \((B, \Sigma)\) at every kept state, so bands integrate over the tightness.

from cultivars.multivariate.large_dim.hierarchical import HierarchicalBVAR

model = HierarchicalBVAR(y, order=2, names=names)
eb = model.fit(method="empirical", n_draws=200, seed=0)
full = model.fit(method="full", n_draws=200, n_burn=200, seed=0)
print(eb.prior_label, eb.hyper_names)
print(eb.hyper_mode.round(3))
print(full.hyper_draws.shape, round(full.acceptance_rate, 2))
print(full.hyperparameter_interval("lambda").round(3))

[0.292 1.696 0.232]
(200, 3) 0.49

Constructor arguments: sum_of_coefficients=True and dummy_initial_observation=True switch the two dummy components (and their hyperparameters) on; decay, exogenous and persistence are held fixed. fit(method="full", n_draws=1000, n_burn=500, seed=None): n_draws is the number of kept draws, n_burn the Metropolis burn-in. The result is a BVARResult subclass with hyper_names, hyper_mode, hyper_draws (empty under empirical Bayes), acceptance_rate (nan under empirical Bayes), method and hyperparameter_interval(name). Its log_marginal_likelihood is \(p(y \mid \hat\theta)\) at the mode, not integrated over the hyperprior.

Non-conjugate priors: GibbsBVAR#

GibbsBVAR states the prior independently of the covariance (Koop and Korobilis 2010),

\[\operatorname{vec}(B) \sim \mathcal{N}(\underline\beta, \underline V), \qquad \Sigma \sim \mathcal{IW}(\underline S, \underline\nu),\]

and samples \(\operatorname{vec}(B) \mid \Sigma\) by one joint GLS draw with precision \(\underline V^{-1} + \Sigma^{-1} \otimes X^\top X\), then \(\Sigma \mid B\) from an inverse-Wishart. Under an adaptive prior a third block redraws the latent scales. Each sweep factors a \((k^2 p + k\,n_{det})\)-square matrix, so wide systems belong to BVAR.

from cultivars.multivariate.large_dim.gibbs import GibbsBVAR
from cultivars.bayes.priors import HorseshoePrior, SpikeAndSlabPrior

gibbs = GibbsBVAR(y, order=1, names=names).fit(n_draws=600, n_burn=200, seed=0)
print(gibbs.prior_label, gibbs.n_draws, gibbs.n_kept)
evidence = gibbs.marginal_likelihood()
print(evidence.method, round(evidence.log_value, 1), evidence.mcse < 0.1)

inw(l1=0.2, l2=0.5, l3=1, l4=100) 600 400
chib -836.9 True

The default prior is IndependentNormalWishartPrior, Litterman’s moments with cross_equation=0.5. The marginal likelihood is Chib’s (1995) estimate from the Gibbs output; it is refused under an adaptive prior and under a prior with dummy observations.

Adaptive shrinkage reports what it learned. A spike-and-slab prior (George, Sun and Ni 2008) gives posterior inclusion probabilities; a global-local prior (horseshoe, Carvalho, Polson and Scott 2010; Dirichlet-Laplace; Normal-Gamma, Griffin and Brown 2010) gives the posterior mean local scale of each standardized coefficient. Both are (p, k, k) stacks aligned with coefficients:

ssvs = GibbsBVAR(y, order=1, names=names, prior=SpikeAndSlabPrior()).fit(
    n_draws=600, n_burn=200, seed=0)
print(ssvs.inclusion_probabilities()[0].round(2))
horseshoe = GibbsBVAR(y, order=1, names=names, prior=HorseshoePrior()).fit(
    n_draws=600, n_burn=200, seed=0)
print(horseshoe.shrinkage_scales()[0].round(2))

[[0.73 0.38 0.39]

Selection reads the sample, not the data-generating process. The true lag matrix is non-zero on the diagonal and at [0, 1] (0.2), [1, 2] and [2, 0] (0.1 each). In this sample least squares puts [0, 2] at 0.23 with a t-ratio of 3.2, so spike-and-slab includes it; the weak [1, 2] link is excluded. The horseshoe scales rank the same way. Calling inclusion_probabilities on a global-local fit, or shrinkage_scales on a spike-and-slab fit, raises SpecificationError. Adaptive priors do not compose: HorseshoePrior() + SumOfCoefficientsPrior() is refused at fit.

fit(n_draws=2000, n_burn=500, thin=1, seed=None): n_draws counts all sweeps, burn-in included, and n_kept is ceil((n_draws - n_burn) / thin). The result adds shrinkage, shrinkage_label, n_draws, n_burn and thin to the shared surface (credible_interval, irf, irf_bands, forecast, forecast_paths, stable_share, convergence).

Fat tails: StudentBVAR#

StudentBVAR keeps the conjugate prior surface and replaces Gaussian innovations with \(u_t \sim t_\nu(0, \Sigma)\), estimated through the scale mixture \(u_t \mid w_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma / w_t)\), \(w_t \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\nu/2, \nu/2)\) (Geweke 1993; Chiu, Mumtaz and Pinter 2017). Given the weights the model is the conjugate VAR on reweighted rows; \(\nu\) is either fixed by the caller or drawn on the grid \(2, \ldots, 29, 30, 35, 40, 50, 60, 80, 100\).

from cultivars.multivariate.large_dim.student import StudentBVAR

rng_t = np.random.default_rng(11)
fat = np.zeros((200, 2))
for t in range(1, 200):
    w = rng_t.gamma(2.0, 0.5)            # t_4 innovations as a scale mixture
    fat[t] = 0.5 * fat[t - 1] + rng_t.standard_normal(2) / np.sqrt(w)
heavy = StudentBVAR(fat, order=1).fit(n_draws=400, n_burn=200, seed=0)
print(heavy.n_kept, heavy.df_estimated)
print(heavy.df_interval().round(1))
print(heavy.outlier_dates().size, heavy.weight_mean.shape)
ratio = heavy.innovation_covariance / heavy.sigma_u
print(np.allclose(ratio, heavy.df / (heavy.df - 2.0)))

Drifting volatility: BVARSV#

BVARSV holds the coefficients constant and lets innovation variances move,

\[u_t = A^{-1} \operatorname{diag}\bigl(e^{h_{1t}/2}, \ldots, e^{h_{kt}/2}\bigr)\, \varepsilon_t, \qquad h_{it} = h_{i,t-1} + \eta_{it},\]

with \(A\) unit lower triangular, after Carriero, Clark and Marcellino (2019). The coefficients are drawn jointly by GLS under a moment prior (default MinnesotaPrior()) and the log variances by the Kim-Shephard-Chib mixture.

from cultivars.multivariate.large_dim.volatility import BVARSV

rng_v = np.random.default_rng(5)
drift = np.zeros((200, 2))
for t in range(1, 200):
    scale = 0.5 + 1.5 * t / 200          # volatility rises through the sample
    drift[t] = 0.5 * drift[t - 1] + scale * rng_v.standard_normal(2)
sv = BVARSV(drift, order=1).fit(n_draws=300, n_burn=100, seed=0)
print(sv.prior_label, sv.n_kept, sv.h_draws.shape)
path = sv.volatility_path("y1")           # (nobs, 3): low, mean, high
print(path.shape, path[0, 1] < path[-1, 1])

fit(n_draws=2000, n_burn=1000, thin=2, k_vol=0.01, seed=None); k_vol is the prior scale of the log-volatility random walk (raise it toward 0.1 when volatility drifts a lot). sigma_u and sigma_draws are the covariance at the end of the sample, \(\Sigma_T = A^{-1} \operatorname{diag}(e^{h_T}) A^{-\top}\), which is what irf and the predictive use; the predictive continues each draw’s random walk forward, so its bands widen with the horizon. The names order is a structural declaration made at estimation time. Dummy-observation priors are refused, and there is no marginal likelihood.

Shrinkage on the point path: VAR(prior=...)#

VAR accepts any prior and returns the posterior mean of the coefficients as a point estimate (Theil-Goldberger mixed estimation, one equation at a time), with their Gaussian posterior covariance in posterior (stderr, conf_int, wald). Each equation’s error variance is held at the univariate residual scale \(s_i^2\), Litterman’s procedure, so those posterior standard deviations do not reflect the VAR’s own residual variance. It is not a sampler: there are no draws, and impulse responses, forecasts and decompositions propagate the point estimate. What it offers in exchange is the whole VARResult surface, including identify, fevd, generalized_fevd and the closed-system contract that Structural identification and Spillover require.

from cultivars.multivariate.reduced_form.vector_autoregression import VAR

ols = VAR(y, order=2, names=names).fit()
shrunk = VAR(y, order=2, names=names, prior=MinnesotaPrior(tightness=0.1)).fit()
print(shrunk.prior_label)
print(round(ols.n_params, 1), round(shrunk.n_params, 1))
conj = VAR(y, order=2, names=names, prior=NormalInverseWishartPrior()).fit()
print(np.allclose(conj.coefficients, res.coefficients))

Under a conjugate prior the point estimate equals BVAR’s exact posterior mean (the last line). n_params charges the effective number of coefficients the prior left free, and llf, aic and bic are evaluated at the posterior mean with that count. The coefficient table in summary() reports posterior standard deviations in place of standard errors.

Convergence#

Every sampled result has convergence(*chains, include=None, exclude=(), rhat_tol=1.01, min_ess=100), which returns a ConvergenceTest over every *_draws array (split R-hat, bulk and tail ESS, MCSE, Geweke). Pass further results from runs with different seeds as extra chains:

second = GibbsBVAR(y, order=1, names=names).fit(n_draws=600, n_burn=200, seed=1)
check = gibbs.convergence(second)
print(check.n_chains, check.n_draws, check.converged)
print(check.worst(2))

How the pieces connect#

  • Evidence. BVAR, HierarchicalBVAR (at the mode) and GibbsBVAR under a static prior return MarginalLikelihoodSelection records from marginal_likelihood(); marginal_likelihood() dispatches to them and compare() ranks them. StudentBVAR and BVARSV have no such method; compare them by predictive score. See The Bayesian workflow.

  • Predictive checks. BVAR, HierarchicalBVAR, GibbsBVAR and StudentBVAR models offer prior_replications; all five results offer posterior_replications, which posterior_predictive_check() consumes. BVARSV has no prior_replications.

  • Forecast evaluation. forecast_paths is the predictive sample that fan_chart(), the density scores and bayesian_model_average() take; see Forecasting and forecast evaluation.

  • Structural analysis. Bayesian VAR results are not closed-system results: they have no identify, ma_representation or fevd, and Spillover refuses them. Orthogonalized irf / irf_bands are the recursive identification in the order of names. For other identification schemes, either rotate beta_draws and sigma_draws yourself or use VAR(prior=...) and Structural identification.

  • Wide panels. BVAR is the shrinkage answer to dimensionality; the selection and factor answers are on Large and high-dimensional systems.

Nuances and gotchas#

  • Adaptive priors are silently mis-used outside GibbsBVAR. BVAR, StudentBVAR, BVARSV and VAR all accept HorseshoePrior() (and the other adaptive priors) without error, but none of them samples the latent scales: they use the prior’s variance at its initial scales as a fixed Gaussian prior, while prior_label still reads horseshoe. BVAR even reports a closed-form marginal likelihood for it. Use adaptive priors with GibbsBVAR only.

  • ``n_draws`` means different things. BVAR and HierarchicalBVAR: draws kept. GibbsBVAR, StudentBVAR, BVARSV: total iterations, burn-in included, then thinned (thin defaults 1, 2, 2). StudentBVAR(...).fit(n_draws=400, n_burn=200) keeps 100 draws.

  • The default prior differs by model. NormalInverseWishartPrior() for BVAR and StudentBVAR, IndependentNormalWishartPrior() for GibbsBVAR, MinnesotaPrior() for BVARSV, least squares for VAR. All centre own first lags at persistence=1.0, the random walk; set persistence=0.0 for growth rates or other stationary data.

  • Dummy priors depend on the data’s level. The sum-of-coefficients rows are scaled by the presample means; on demeaned data or growth rates they carry almost no weight whatever tightness is. tightness in the package means larger is tighter; the Giannone-Lenza-Primiceri \(\mu\) and \(\delta\) used by HierarchicalBVAR are reciprocals (larger is looser).

  • Composition double counts. NormalInverseWishartPrior() + NormalInverseWishartPrior() is accepted and precision-weights the two, halving every variance. MinnesotaPrior(sum_of_coefficients=1.0) + SumOfCoefficientsPrior() adds the same rows twice.

  • Orthogonalized responses depend on column order, for every model here. BVARResult.irf and HierarchicalBVARResult.irf take only horizon, orthogonalized and cumulative; the GibbsBVAR, StudentBVAR and BVARSV results additionally take shocks= and responses= name subsets to bound memory.

  • Bands are pointwise 68%. (16th, mean, 84th) at each cell, not a joint band over horizons; the middle value is a Monte Carlo mean, not beta_mean. Take other quantiles from beta_draws or forecast_paths.

  • Wide systems. BVAR needs more rows than columns and at least order + 2 rows, nothing more, so 20 variables at two lags fit on 40 observations. lag_order_selection on the same model is computed by least squares and refuses max_lags beyond what an unrestricted VAR supports on that sample; compare marginal likelihoods on a trimmed common sample instead (above).

  • Random-walk priors put mass past the unit circle. On persistent data stable_share is often well below one; long-horizon responses and forecasts then include explosive draws.

  • The hierarchical evidence is optimistic. HierarchicalBVAR reports \(p(y \mid \hat\theta)\) at the maximizing hyperparameters; putting it in a compare table beside fixed-prior fits favours it by construction.

  • ``compare`` warns, it does not refuse. Records with different nobs produce a note under the table; the probabilities are still printed.
